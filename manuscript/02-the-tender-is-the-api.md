Perhaps the procurement process for construction should be redescribed in terms of software development. There are three main components: the design; the tender, which is the API; and the compilation, which is construction itself. The design and the compilation negotiate through the API of the tender process. During an auction-styled event, the builder agrees to provide all the resources needed to compile the design.

Readers who know the software world will recognize the API mandate at Amazon in 2002, as Steve Yegge told it years later in a post that escaped onto the open internet. Every team will expose its data and its functions through a service interface. No team will reach into another team's code. Anyone who does not comply will be fired. Applied to construction, the teams are a design consultant and a construction firm, and the API contract is a tender package.

The virtue of an interface is that nobody has to read the other side's source code. In software, one team does not open another team's code to find out what it does. It calls the interface and trusts the answer, and it can do that because the interface is written down and every call is checked against what was written. A tender tries to do the same thing. The designer need not know how the builder schedules its crews, and the builder need not know why the engineer chose that beam. The bill of quantities is the call every bidder makes, and the bid is the answer that comes back.

Then look at what the interface is made of. Bot-based procurement assumes that PDFs will be phased out. They are an archaic artifact from the world of paper. The file sizes are enormous and they are not reliably read by machines. They lack well-formatted metadata for rapid search and bot instruction, and they display poorly on a screen, especially on the cell phones used by frontline workers. The zombie garden said why the file survives anyway: it is the stamped and frozen copy that a court reads. The tender's interface is a stack of such copies. In software terms, the contract is written on the one medium the compiler cannot read.

The design side is not spared. BIM modelling, AI review and licensed professionals help screen for errors, but bug-free drawings are still a mythical beast. Every other API in the world is tested by calling it, thousands of times, before anyone depends on it. A tender is called once, by each bidder, in the few weeks before the bids close, and the errors it hides are found by the people least placed to say so. That is the divorce of chapter 5.

Sufficiently complex building models equal specifications. A BIM model with all the product information encoded in the code used to generate the model would be a potential source for bot procurement. In bot procurement, the model would render for humans, but the bots would probably prefer the files. The challenges are significantly higher costs and a higher risk to the designer: paper can be reviewed by eye far more easily than the code used to generate a high-rise. The interface already exists in several countries, where the bid travels as a file the owner writes and the bidder fills in. A later chapter visits them. This chapter stays with what the metaphor is for, which is the traffic through the door.

## The cost of a call

Every interface has a price per call, even when nobody writes it down. For a tender, the price is what it costs a firm to read the documents, measure the work, price it, and write up the parts the buyer intends to score. For most of the industry's history that price was high, and it shaped the market more than any rule did.

Public sector specifications are complex and the contract clauses are one-sided, and there is a reason. Since the public sector cannot "profit" from success, but does need to answer hard questions when things go wrong, the terms and conditions are fairly onerous to bidders. Taking the time to thoroughly review and understand the project requirements, and then to prepare a bid package for technical evaluation, when the odds are that this will be a sunk cost, makes for low bidder turnout.

The auditors agree. The European Court of Auditors found that between 2011 and 2021 the share of public contracts with a single bidder nearly doubled, to 42 percent, while the average number of bidders fell from about six to three. It named the causes: administrative burden, over-restrictive criteria, tailor-made specifications and market concentration. Ontario's Auditor General found that the firms answering the province's calls for major projects fell from more than five in 2014 to about two and a half, and that four of thirty public-private partnerships, each worth over a billion dollars, drew a single bid. The one place a buyer pays for the call outright is American design-build. There, federal rules let an agency pay the losing teams a stipend, usually a third to a half of what the proposal cost them, and the rules name the reason: to encourage competition and keep smaller companies from being put at a disadvantage.

Think of that cost as a large oak. For a century it stood over the market and shaded out everyone who could not afford to grow under it. Only a firm with an estimating department, a bonding line and the patience to lose five bids in six could live in its shade. The buyer's systems grew up in that shade too. The plan room, the three-week tender period and the evaluation committee were built for the mature forest under one tree: a few large bidders, a manageable stack of bids, and time to read them.

## The oak falls

Then AI made the call nearly free. AI fixes the cost of bidding, so a standardized form of AI procurement is needed to manage the excess bids.

The flood is easiest to see away from construction, where it arrived first. In February 2023 the science fiction magazine Clarkesworld closed its submissions after about five hundred machine-written stories arrived in three weeks alongside seven hundred real ones. In 2026 the arXiv preprint server, after a record forty thousand papers in a month, capped every author at two submissions a month and tightened who may vouch for a newcomer. About 40 percent of the proposals firms sent to the European Union's research programme showed signs of AI editing by the end of 2024, with the youngest and least experienced applicants adopting it fastest. One Irish evaluator of public-service tenders saw competitions that drew three or four bids in 2023 draw twelve to fifteen. In construction's priced bids the flood has not been measured yet, and this chapter will come to the reason.

Picture a large oak falling over, and a thicket of plants competing for the new sunlight. AI has powered a rush for the light. There are more participants than can be counted, and the old systems, set up for the mature forest, are unable to cope. The interplay between bidders, software vendors and procurement is in flux, with the ecosystem's slack being rapidly colonized by new species.

Emmett Shear once put the physics of this in a short note. Spare energy does not stay spare. It gets used up as fast as the system allows, by things that grow, copy themselves and search for better versions of themselves, which is as good a definition of life as any. The cost of bidding was stored energy. For decades it kept most of the industry out of the tender. Released, it feeds whatever grows fastest, and what grows fastest right now is anything that writes bids.

## An explosion in liveness

This is an explosion in liveness. Chapter 4 used the word in the engineer's sense: a protocol owes its users safety, the promise that nothing bad will happen, and liveness, the promise that something good eventually will. Venkatesh Rao gives it a wider meaning. A thing is live, he writes, when time tells memory how to grow, and memory tells time which way to point. In plain words, a market is alive when what happens next changes what it remembers, and what it remembers changes what happens next. A plan room full of frozen PDFs has no liveness by either definition. A tender market with a thousand new bidders has plenty, and that is the problem.

Rao adds a warning that matters more than the definition. Signs of life are not life. A heartbeat on a monitor and the uptime on a dashboard are fingers pointing at the moon. For a century a polished bid was a sign of life, because writing one took effort and knowledge of the work. Two economists who studied a freelance platform before and after AI writing tools arrived watched that sign die. Employers had paid a premium for applications tailored to the job. Once tailoring became free, the premium vanished, the workers who had been hired most were hired less, and the weakest were hired more. The polished bid is now only a sign. The market has never been more alive, and its bids have never said less.

If the sign is gone, what is left is memory. A market's memory is its bid history: what this kind of work has cost, on this kind of job, in this region, under these conditions, read against every new price. That memory is what a buyer can hold a thousand bids up against, and it is the one thing a bid-writing bot cannot polish, because it did not write it.

Rao's last warning is the one a standard has to obey: when the entity captures the liveness, the liveness dies. If one vendor owns the market's memory, it stops being the market's. A study of hiring found that a screening model favours résumés written by the same model, shortlisting them up to 60 percent more often. Put the same bot on both sides of a tender and the market's memory becomes one firm's opinion of itself. The thicket would then be thinned by the one plant that owns the sun. Keep the memory public, and keep it plural.

## Slack, and the other option

The organization theorist Jay Galbraith wrote, in the late 1960s and the 1970s, that an organization facing more uncertainty than it can process has two choices. It can add slack: more time, more money, more cushion between the parts, so that nobody has to know everything. Or it can raise its capacity to process information, with better systems and more direct lines between the people who need to talk. Our work would raise the organizational capacity to process information. This feels like the correct approach, since the current system of PDFs and digital drawings does not seem to produce any slack over the use of the paper plan room. They did raise processing capacity by removing the need to travel to bid, but the actual attention needed remained the same.

Construction's answer to uncertainty has always been slack. The tender period is long, the bid carries a cushion for the errors in the drawings, and the contingency carries a cushion for the rest. The oak was slack too: a cost so high that only a few could pay it, which kept the stack of bids small enough for a committee to read. AI did not remove the uncertainty. It removed the slack, and left the attention needed exactly where it was. The thicket is slack released without the capacity to meet it.

A public buyer cannot simply hire. In the public sector, the need to prevent nepotism and bid-fixing, while offering the opportunity to bid to the widest possible array of the population, is served by the increased number of bidders. But increasing staffing for human review may prove to be politically sensitive. The public would rightfully ask: why don't the public servants use AI to evaluate the bids? The answer would be too technical. The probabilistic nature of AI judgements would lead to significant legal issues requiring legislative changes, which would open the door to nepotism by giving public sector employees more say in who can bid. Without a standard, AI scoring would invite a claim. The first such claim is already in a court: a losing bidder on a US Army contract worth about $450 million says the evaluators' AI tool invented a weakness in its proposal.

The standard is the answer to this issue. The standard solves it by being Galbraith's improved capacity. It would allow more data to move faster, improving the velocity of construction firms and of the buyers that use their services, for net increases in industry-wide productivity.

## Four ways to answer a flooded door

Software has met this flood before, and it has four answers to a flooded interface. It can limit how often anyone may call, which engineers call a rate limit and this chapter will call a cap. It can decide who may call, with a key. It can charge for each call, a fee. Or it can check every call against rules that everyone can read, a published check. Construction has a twin for each, and the twins show which answers a public buyer can live with.

Caps. Clarkesworld's closed door was a cap, and it turned away seven hundred real stories to stop five hundred fakes. arXiv's two papers a month fall on established authors too. Construction's cap is the shortlist, and the trade rules allow it only when it is announced in advance, with the criteria stated. A cap hits good and bad callers alike.

Keys. A key says who sent the request, not whether it is right. arXiv tightened who may vouch for a newcomer; construction has prequalification and capacity ratings. The trade rules already bar a public buyer from requiring prior contracts, because tight keys favour the firms already inside. A private buyer can decide that they are willing to pay a premium to only consider established companies. The public sector would find itself in a conflict of principles.

Fees. Making the sender pay is the oldest idea in the fight against junk mail, proposed in 1992, and the most carefully studied failure. Ben Laurie and Richard Clayton worked out in 2004 what a price high enough to stop spam would do to everyone else. It would hold legitimate senders to about the global average of normal use, and it would block a measurable share of an internet provider's honest customers. Their conclusion was blunt: proof of work will not be a solution to spam. Construction's fee is the bid bond. It does screen out desperate bids, and the United Nations' guide to its model procurement law notes that participation falls wherever a charge is levied. Several countries swap the bond for a signed declaration below a threshold so that small contractors can bid at all. The fee falls hardest on the newcomers a public buyer is supposed to welcome.

Each of the first three answers helps one party at another's expense. The buyer gets less to read, and the newcomer gets the door.

The fourth answer is the only one that scales without shutting anyone out, and email is again the proof. From 2024, two of the largest mail providers required every bulk sender to authenticate under public standards. Unauthenticated mail reaching their users fell by about 65 percent, some 265 billion messages in a year, and legitimate senders were spared the fee that would have blocked some of them. Two pieces of theory say why a published check wins as the flood grows. Louis Kaplow showed in 1992 that a rule costs more to write once and less each time it is applied, while judgment costs the same every time, so the more often a question comes up, the better a rule pays. A flood is that frequency. And *Noise*, by Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein, reports an insurer that asked its underwriters to price identical cases: they differed by a median of 55 percent, where the executives had expected about ten. Bids judged case by case carry the same noise, and a protest is a bidder who has noticed.

Highway construction, as it happens, already has all four answers in rough form: prequalification, a bid bond, a bid file the owner writes and the bidder fills in, and bid reviews with written reasons. That may be why priced highway bids have not flooded, and why nobody has measured a flood there. The written parts of a bid, the qualifications, the method statements and the technical proposals, have none of the four, and that is where the thicket is growing.

The check has one more rule to obey. Paul Graham's spam filter of 2002 worked because spam had to say spammy things. To beat it, he wrote, spammers would have to make their mail indistinguishable from yours. AI-written bids already are. So the check cannot be a check of the prose. It has to look at what the prose cannot change: the price, read against the market's memory, and the claims, pointed at evidence.

## What thins the thicket

A thicket does not stay a thicket. Something thins it. In a forest it is the first plants to reach the canopy, which shade out the rest until the forest is mature again, with new trees in it. In the tender it will be one of three things. Noise, where the buyer reads a sample and the award becomes a lottery with better prose. Capture, where one vendor's bot reads for the buyer and writes for the bidders, and the market's memory becomes its property. Or a published check: the same test for every bid, the same answer for the same bid, its miss rate measured and public, kept by nobody who sells bids. The first wastes the liveness, the second kills it, and only the third lets the market stay alive and still be read.

Even the third needs upkeep. The economists who costed spam warned that every proposal fails if it assumes away the spammers' ability to adapt. Bidders will learn any check that is published, which is the point of publishing it, and the check's miss rate has to be measured again as they do.

## A point of convergence

Part of the motivation for the project comes from the need for tools to help surgical teams, in Fred Brooks's sense, work together while maintaining their status as principals on larger projects. While the market for the idea may currently be found in larger companies with more bids to place, once deployed the tech should filter through the industry to benefit the decentralized teams. This points to the need to build a protocol to balance the tensions between software providers, large bidders and owners. They need a point of convergence if the project is to gain the consensus needed for large-scale adoption.

The three short chapters that follow take the parties one at a time: the buyers, whose door is flooded; the bidders, who are the thicket; and the vendors, who sell shovels to both and would like to own the sun. Each gets the same three questions. Who are they, what do they need, and what does a published check do for them that the other three answers cannot?

One honest paragraph before the parties. Whether redescribing procurement as software development holds up as more than a useful metaphor is the deepest open question in the project, and the one tested least. An API returns the same answer to the same call. A tender returns a different price from every bidder, and that difference is the information the buyer paid for. The metaphor holds at the door and fails at the answer. That is why the standard the project asks for is a check on the call, whether the bid read the same documents, priced the same quantities and made claims that can be traced, and not a bot that answers the call. Decomposing a project into requirements for one bid may be the equivalent of using PDFs in the age of scrolling. If so, the interface of the future is smaller calls, made by more teams, checked the same way. The oak will not grow back. The question is who gets the light.
