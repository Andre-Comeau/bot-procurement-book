# Appendix C. Glossary

## Terms

*Each term names the chapter where it is used most.*

**Bid bond.** A guarantee, usually from a bonding company, that a bidder who wins will sign the contract at the price it bid. It is one of the four answers highway construction already has to a flood of bids (chapter 1).

**Bid cushion.** The money a bidder adds to cover what it could not check in the time it had: errors in the drawings, unclear quantities, risk it cannot price. The book calls it slack (chapter 1).

**Bid depository.** An office that receives trade contractors' sealed prices on bid day, stamps them with its own clock and passes them to the general contractors bidding the job (chapter 9).

**Bid spread.** The gap between the lowest bid and the next one. Bonding companies read it first: one large bonding company asks for a letter of explanation when the next bid is more than 10 percent higher (chapters 3 and 13).

**Bill of quantities.** A list of every item of work in a tender with its measured quantity, so that each bidder prices the same list. Highway agencies call the items pay items (chapters 1, 5 and 17).

**Bonding company.** The book's plain word for a surety: the company that issues a builder's bid, performance and payment bonds. Chapter 13, written for them, uses their own word (see Surety).

**Bot admin.** The project's name for the far world where machines keep track of the work and the documents, not the people. It replaced "bot monitoring" (chapters 9 and 16).

**Bot Construction.** The wider field: construction arranged, priced, run and eventually built with bots, from documents written for machines to robots on site (prologue).

**Bot Procurement.** This book, and the proposal inside it: the part of Bot Construction kept small enough to test first, which asks what price a bot puts on a tender and who is liable when it gets it wrong (prologue).

**Buyer as insurer.** The base case of the book's plan: the buyer runs the standard bot, publishes its number and carries the loss if the number was wrong, so no insurer is needed (chapter 3).

**Cap.** One of the four answers to a flood of requests: a limit on how many anyone may send. Construction's cap is the shortlist (chapter 1).

**Change order.** A signed change to the work after the contract is let, with its price. Every change is a purchase from a supplier of one, because only the builder already on site can do it (chapter 16).

**Checking the estimator.** The estimator learned from Texas contracts let before May 2026 and was checked, version by version, on contracts let after, which it had not learned from. Because each version was chosen knowing how the last one scored, those scores probably flatter it a little. A check on newer contracts, left alone so far, will be run once there is a product and someone who wants the answer (chapter 16).

**Construction Manager.** A builder hired early, for its qualifications, to advise on the design and then manage the building of it, usually at a price agreed with open books (chapter 4).

**Cost plus.** A contract that pays the builder what the work costs plus a fee, instead of a price fixed in advance (chapter 4).

**Design-Bid-Build.** The traditional order: the owner's designers finish the drawings, builders bid on them, and the winner builds what was drawn (chapter 4).

**Design-Build.** One firm both designs and builds, under one contract. In progressive Design-Build the team is chosen for its qualifications and the price is agreed in packages as the design firms up (chapter 4).

**Engineer's estimate.** The price the buyer's own engineers put on the work before the tender. It is the price the estimator will be measured against (chapter 16).

**Estimator.** A bot the project is building that will put a price on each line of a tender from what bidders have charged before, as a second opinion before the tender closes (chapter 16).

**Evaluator.** The estimator pointed the other way: a tool that will check the price of a change after the contract is let, draft the memo that says whether it is fair and reasonable, and hand it to a person to sign (chapter 16).

**Fair and reasonable.** The test a public buyer has to meet, in writing, before paying a price nobody competed for, such as the price of a change (chapter 16).

**Fee.** One of the four answers to a flood of requests: making each sender pay. Construction's nearest twin is the bid bond and the cost of preparing a bid (chapter 1).

**Flag.** A mark the estimator or the evaluator will put on a line it has seen too rarely to price with confidence, so that a person prices it instead. A flag only works if someone has to act on it (chapter 16).

**General Contractor.** The builder that holds the contract with the owner and hires the trade contractors who do most of the work (chapter 4).

**Hard bid.** A tender won on price alone, usually a lump sum, by the lowest bidder that meets the terms (chapter 4).

**Jig, jigbot.** A jig holds the work and the tool in the right relation so a hard cut can be made again, safely, by anyone on the crew. A jigbot is what a tradesperson will build when they set up and teach a robot for the task at hand, the way they would build a jig (chapter 9).

**Key.** One of the four answers to a flood of requests: deciding who may send one. Construction's keys are prequalification and capacity ratings (chapter 1).

**Keyholder.** The named person who reviews what a bot drafted, adjusts the flagged lines, decides and signs, with a reason (chapters 16 and 17a).

**Listener.** A bot that collects bidders' questions during a tender, from people or bots, and answers with no more than a nod, a shake or a shrug (chapter 17).

**Lump sum.** One price for the whole of the work, or for a few large parts, instead of a price for each line (chapters 4 and 16).

**Margin insurance.** The bidder's cover in the book's plan: a policy that would pay part of the shortfall when the average margin on a bidder's covered contracts falls below its target. No insurer offers it yet (chapter 3 and Appendix E).

**Patent and latent errors.** A patent error is one a careful bidder should have spotted in the tender; a latent one could not have been seen. A bidder who should have seen a patent error and did not ask usually cannot charge for it later (chapter 17a).

**PDF zombie.** The tender document that died as a technology but kept walking as a protocol, because it carries the seal and the signatures the law still asks for (chapters 1, 5 and 15).

**Pinned and published.** Two kinds of specification: pinned parts are the must-have and critical systems, fixed in detail; published parts set a baseline to guide bidders without fixing how the work is done (chapter 17).

**Prequalification.** Checking that a bidder has the capacity and record for the work before it may bid (chapters 1 and 7).

**Published check.** One of the four answers to a flood of requests: checking every request against rules that everyone can read. It is the answer this book builds on (chapter 1).

**Reasonable bot.** The bot a judge would compare others against when asking what a careful reader should have understood from a tender, the way the law already asks what a reasonable bidder would have understood (prologue and chapter 14).

**Record.** What is kept each time a bot's reading is relied on: the tender, the version of the standard it was read against, and the reading, so a court can check it later. It is a clause in the tool test and the sign-off rather than a standard of its own (chapter 17).

**Second opinion.** What the estimator and the evaluator will offer: a price drawn from past tenders, beside the price the buyer is being asked to pay, for a person to weigh (chapter 16).

**Sign-off.** The third of the book's three standards: before relying on a bot's reading of a tender, a named person confirms the bot passed the test for that kind of work, read the version of the documents that governs, and had what it found looked at by someone able to overrule it, then puts their name to the reading (chapter 17).

**Slack.** Time, money and cushion kept between the parts of a system so that nobody has to know everything. Construction has always chosen slack; the book asks for more capacity to read instead (chapter 1).

**Standard bot.** A proposal: a published bot that reads a tender the way the standard says it should be read, so anyone can rerun it and a judge can measure other bots against it (chapters 14, 15 and 17).

**Stipend.** A payment from the buyer to the losing bidders on a large job, to cover part of what their bids cost to prepare (chapter 1).

**Surety.** The industry's word for a bonding company: a company that guarantees a builder's promise to the buyer, through a bid bond and, if the builder wins, performance and payment bonds. The builder and its owners promise to repay any loss, so a surety underwrites the firm rather than the price (chapter 13).

**Template.** A tender's items, units and quantities written as plain data beside the PDF, so a bot can read them without guessing and a person can read them without software (chapter 17).

**Tender.** The package a buyer issues to invite priced offers for construction work, and the competition that follows until the bids are opened. The book's first chapter treats it as an interface between the buyer and everyone who bids (chapter 1).

**Thicket.** The crowd of new bids that springs up once bidding becomes nearly free, the way a thicket fills the clearing where an old oak fell (chapters 1 and 15).

**Three standards.** The book's proposal for the documents a bot reads (the template), the test a pricing bot sits (the tool test) and the person who signs for what it found (the sign-off) (chapter 17).

**Tool test.** The second of the three standards: a public set of tenders a bot has never seen, one fixed way of scoring its prices against what bidders bid, and a published miss rate with a tolerance. Drafted now and fixed when the estimator has earned it (chapter 17).

**Witness and Oracle.** A pair of bots inside any buyer-side system: the Witness reads the full records, and the Oracle checks what leaves, so the bot that faces outward never touches the files (chapter 16).

## Acronyms

- **AACE.** AACE International, which publishes the estimate classes used to say how accurate an estimate can be at each stage of design.
- **AI.** Artificial intelligence; in this book, mostly the language models that read and write text.
- **BIM.** Building information modelling: a shared digital model of a building or structure.
- **CCDC.** Canadian Construction Documents Committee, which publishes Canada's standard construction contracts.
- **FAR.** The US Federal Acquisition Regulation.
- **GAEB.** The German standard for exchanging bills of quantities as data.
- **IDS.** Information Delivery Specification, a buildingSMART format that says what information a model must contain.
- **IFC, IFCX.** Industry Foundation Classes, the open format for building models; IFCX is the next version, being written as small JSON records.
- **ISO 10845.** The international standard for construction procurement procedure.
- **OCDS, OC4IDS.** The Open Contracting Data Standard, for publishing public contracts as data, and its version for infrastructure projects.
- **RFI.** Request for information: a question from the builder about the drawings or specifications.
