# One bot failure, from tender to court

## The bot in the witness box

Here is a case that has not happened yet. A town tenders a road with an excavation line whose quantity is twice what the ground will need. Three bidders' bots read the tender. One of them spots the error and its bidder prices the line cheap, the way the Texas bidders in chapter 2 did, and says nothing. The buyer's bot that collects questions gathers one about the neighbouring line and misses this one. The job is let, the quantity is short, and the winning bidder makes up the difference elsewhere. A losing bidder sues, saying the tender was wrong and the winner knew it.

The court asks an old question in a new form. In the United States a bidder who should have seen a patent error, and did not ask, usually cannot charge for it later. "Should have seen" is measured against a reasonable bidder. Once a reasonable bidder's bot would have found the error, the error is patent. No new duty has been written. The old one simply bites harder.

To answer, the court needs four things. The first is a reasonable reader: a published version of the standard bot that anyone can run again on the same tender and get the same reading. A hosted model that changes underneath its users cannot do this, and a bot with pinned weights can. The second is how often that reader is wrong, measured on contracts it never saw. Courts already ask for this of expert evidence, through Daubert in the United States and the cases that followed Mohan in Canada: a method that can be tested and a known rate of error. A reading that anyone can run again meets the first of those before anyone has agreed on a tolerance. The third is a trail showing who read which version of the tender, and when. The fourth is a person.

The case separates two things this book has sometimes run together. Accuracy is the estimator's job, and it is measured in percent. Defensibility is the standard's job, and it is measured under cross-examination. A bot can be accurate and still be useless in court if nobody can rerun it, and a bot with a modest record can be good evidence if its misses are known.

Liability then lands where the earlier chapters said it should. The design sits with the designer, the reading with each bidder's bot, and the addendum with whoever issued it. That holds only if each of the four exists. Without them, the court is left with three bidders' word against each other and a bot nobody can call back.

The last witness is the keyholder: the person who chose to rely on the reading and signed. The Army case in chapter 1 shows the gap. The court ordered the AI tool's output into the record, but it could not tell what the tool had done, and the account of who had seen it changed in the middle of the case. A standard cannot make the bot a defendant. It can make sure there is always someone in the box who can explain why they trusted it.
