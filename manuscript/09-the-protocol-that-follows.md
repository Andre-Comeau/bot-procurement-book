# The protocol that follows

The tool exists. In rehearsal checks on public Texas bid data, the estimator missed the lowest bidders' prices by a median of 21 percent line by line, against 29 percent for the state's own engineer's estimate, and chapter 10 showed what an evaluator built on it could save a buyer after the tender. That chapter ended at a door, and behind it was the standard, World 2 on the map. The last chapter put a bot in the witness box and found what a court would ask of it: a record of what the bot did, when and why, and a reasonable reader to measure it against.

Until now, the plan for getting through that door rested on one assumption: build a model that is good enough, publish it, and the field would gather around it on its own. The estimator would be the seed and the rest would grow.

That is not how standards get made, and the people who have made them say so plainly. So this chapter does something more practical. It proposes three standards: one for the documents a bot reads, one to test the bot, and one for the person who signs for what it found. All three are addressed to the buyers, designers, bidders and lawyers who already run tenders, because they are the ones who would adopt them. Then it follows each to the existing standards that could carry it.

## Standards make the world

In 1875 the Pennsylvania Railroad hired a chemist. Charles Dudley was fresh from a doctorate at Yale, and the railroad put him in charge of a new laboratory in Altoona, Pennsylvania. His job was to find out what the company was buying.

Mostly it was buying steel rails. Dudley studied how different rails held up in service, and in 1878 he published the result: rails of milder steel lasted longer than rails of hard steel. He proposed a better recipe, and the railroad turned it into specifications for its suppliers.

The steel makers did not take it well. ASTM's own history says the report "raised a firestorm among steel masters", who saw a customer's specification as meddling in their trade. Some told the railroads that if they did not take the rails on offer, they would not get any.

Dudley's answer was not a louder specification. A good one, he came to think, needs two kinds of knowledge: how a product behaves in the mill, which the seller has, and how it behaves in service, which the buyer has. In 1898 he was among the seventy people who met in Philadelphia to start the American section of an international testing association. In 1902 it became the American Society for Testing Materials, with Dudley as its first president. Its committees were built so that sellers could not outnumber buyers and could not take the chair. The test was written together, and nobody had to take anyone's word for it.

That is this project's story, more than a century early. A buyer measured what it was being sold, published the result, met the sellers' resistance, and then built a place where both sides write the test. Rails came first. The project would like prices to follow.

David Lang came to standards the way Dudley did, by needing one. His open-source underwater robot, OpenROV, taught its team that the bottleneck in ocean technology was the connector, not the robot. His essay for the Summer of Protocols, the programme that became the Protocol Institute, "Standards Make the World", is the history he went looking for.

A standard that makes the world, rather than ruling it, had to be made first, by somebody. Lang's answer to who that somebody is: standards "are made and remade by (usually small) groups of people", and "standards-making is something that anyone can engage in, even though almost no one thinks to do it."

He describes two paths. The formal one has numbered steps: a proposal, a draft by experts, public comment, a revision, a vote and publication. The other is the Internet's, summed up in David Clark's line about the IETF, "rough consensus and running code": build something that works, get people using it, and govern it afterwards. MIDI, USB and the CubeSat each began with a small group saying, in Lang's words, "we're doing this—are you coming?", and nearly all ended up in a consortium or a standards body. The disruptive path does not skip the committee. It arrives with something already running.

The formal path has a famous failure. In 1977 the International Organization for Standardization set up a committee to design Open Systems Interconnection, OSI for short, a complete plan for how computers would talk to each other. The rules let any interested party take part, and when two camps of engineers disagreed, the committee kept both designs, so the plan grew larger and slower. In 1988 the US Department of Commerce required government agencies to buy equipment that used it. Anyone who wanted to read the standards had to buy paper copies from ISO, one copy at a time. The Internet's own protocols, TCP/IP, were free, and from January 1983 the contractors of the American defence research agency had to use them to stay on its network. By the early 1990s OSI had all but stalled. One of the Internet's advocates, Einar Stefferud, said it best: "OSI is a beautiful dream, and TCP/IP is living it!"

That is why this project is building its estimator, and the model behind it, before it fixes the standard. A standard written first, by committee, for everyone, can be complete and still never run. The project would rather arrive at the committee with working parts: a tool already checked against public bids, a draft of the documents it reads, and a test written so that anyone can run it.

Construction procurement has walked both paths before. Ron Watermeyer built an international procurement standard on South Africa's national ones, which already worked, and ISO 10845 followed in 2010. The Open Contracting Data Standard went the other way round: a small team wrote a data format for publishing public contracts and let governments pick it up one at a time. Neither computes anything. ISO 10845 fixes the procedure and OCDS the record of the contract, and both stop at the edge of the question this book asks.

## The ally that stopped at the tender

Of all the standards on the map, the Open Contracting Data Standard is the one this project looks at hardest, and the reason is what it leaves out.

OCDS follows a public contract from start to finish. It gives each contracting process an identifier and a shape that runs from the plan through the tender, the award and the contract to the payments. The infrastructure version, written with CoST in 2019, adds the project itself and the changes to its contracts, with their reasons. Most of the frame a bot would need around a construction tender is already built, in a format governments publish.

What OCDS does not carry is the tender itself. A tender in OCDS points to its documents, and those documents are still the drawings and specifications that bidders price, usually as PDFs. There is room for items, quantities and unit prices, but few publishers fill it in, and those are the numbers a price check needs.

Stopping there was probably wise. The tender documents are where each country keeps its own law, trade customs and units of measure, and a standard written for every government at once had good reason to leave them alone. This project goes deeper because its question lives in those documents. A bot that judges a price has to read what was priced.

That makes OCDS an ally rather than a rival. Its team shipped something that worked and let governments join one at a time without waiting for perfection, and with modern tools, having something that works may now matter more than having something perfect. The three standards this chapter proposes can hang off the frame OCDS built instead of building a second one beside it.

## Three standards

That question is who is liable when a bot causes harm, and it decides what the standard is for. Someone will have to say what a reasonable bot would have read in the tender, and someone will have to answer for acting on it. The standard is a legal instrument before it is a technical one: nobody is asked to trust the bot, only given a reasonable reader to cite in court and a person who answers for the reading. That also moves the project out of the innovation bucket, where public buyers are wary, and into the standards bucket, where they already know how to take part.

One standard cannot carry all of that, and the older bodies never tried. ASTM tests the rail; it does not tell the railroad's engineer what to sign before laying it. That takes the kind of standard a profession writes for its own members. Bot Procurement needs both, and a third beneath them, because neither a test nor a signature means much if the documents can be read two ways.

**The first standard is for the documents.** It replaces the PDF as the copy a bot reads: a tender package written as plain text, with Markdown for the parts people read and JSON for the parts machines count. Every requirement carries an identifier, every pay item a name from a shared list, and every addendum a new version of the file rather than a new pile of pages. A pay item might read like this:

```json
{"item": "Excavation, common", "unit": "m3", "quantity": 1250,
 "requirement": "02-310-3.2", "version": "addendum 2"}
```

A bidder's bot can read it without guessing, a person can read it without software, and two versions can be compared line by line. The PDF does not have to die on the first day. It can keep the seal for a while, as the stamped copy a court reads, with the template issued beside it "for reading". The order of precedence flips only when the villagers are ready, and that flip is the moment the zombie is finally laid to rest.

**The second standard is the tool test.** It is Dudley's half of the problem: measure what is being sold, in a way the seller cannot argue with. For a pricing bot, that means a public set of tenders it has never seen, one fixed way of scoring its prices against what bidders actually bid, and a published miss rate with a tolerance around it. It is written backwards from what it has to catch, so the project's own estimator will be only the first tool to sit it, and a second vendor's bot can pass it too. What the tolerance should be is a question for evidence, not for a drafting committee, and the estimator is being built to answer it. A tolerance written today would be a guess with a version number, so this standard is drafted now and fixed when the estimator has earned it.

**The third standard is the sign-off.** It binds the person, not the tool. American actuaries already work this way. Their standards board writes standards of practice through public exposure drafts, and one of them, ASOP 56, covers models. It does not tell an actuary which model to use. It tells them what to understand, check and disclose before relying on one, including one somebody else built, and an actuary who skips it answers to the profession. Surveyors have followed for AI: since March 2026, a member of the Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors must decide, by name, whether an AI output is reliable enough to use. The sign-off standard asks the same of whoever relies on a bot's reading of a tender: the buyer's evaluator, the bidder's estimator, the designer checking that a bot read the drawings as intended. Before signing, they confirm that the bot passed the test for this kind of work, that it read the version of the documents that governs, and that someone able to overrule it looked at what it found. Then their name goes on the reading. A flag nobody owns is a flag nobody reads.

The record of what the bot read, by which version and when, needs no standard of its own. Logging is cheap for a machine, so it becomes a clause in the other two: without it the test cannot be rerun and the sign-off has nothing to point at.

A court can work with two of the three long before anyone agrees on a tolerance: the documents show what was asked, and the sign-off shows who relied on the answer and what they checked. A party that can show which version its bot read and who signed for it has less to explain than one that cannot. The test, when it comes, adds the known error rate.

That sets the order of the standards: the documents now, the sign-off as soon as anyone relies on a bot, which is already, and the test when the estimator has earned it.

## Where the standards land

None of the three needs a new institution to start. Each can ride on a standard that already exists, kept by people who already meet. The map of World 2 shows them in the order they can be started.

From World 1 a path crosses eight numbered levels, each with a note. 2-1, field building: name it loudly, define it slowly, gather a small group. 2-2, OCDS and OC4IDS: an extension for the tender's questions, addenda and readings. 2-3, IFC 4.3 and IDS: requirements a bot can check before the bid, not after. 2-4, measurement: one name for each pay item, so prices can be compared. The path crosses a river by a bridge to 2-5, CCDC 23: a guide note naming the bot version the tender was read by. 2-6, ISO 10845-3: conditions of tender, with a bot's question answered to every bidder. 2-7, AACE classes: a conformance class beside the five estimate classes. 2-8, IEEE 3119: a construction profile, to buy bots that pass the public test. The path ends at a castle, a new part of ISO 10845 for the reasonable bot (a public test set, a tolerance and a required record), and past it a courthouse, the first case (Daubert, Mohan and the Rule 707 redraft: a method and a known error rate). The caption reads "Every level is a standard that already exists. The castle is the one that doesn't."](../images/world-2-standards.png)

**Where the documents go.** The pay-item names need a shared list, and the lists already exist: a civil engineering method of measurement like CESMM4, or a state transportation department's catalogue of pay items. The template needs a published crosswalk to them, not a new method. On the cost side, RICS publishes a free data standard, in JSON and XML, that already carries the international cost classification, so the template can borrow its names. On the design side, IFC 4.3 already covers roads, rail and bridges, and buildingSMART's Information Delivery Specification lets a designer state requirements a program can check, which would tie the template's requirement identifiers to the model. In Canada, CCDC 23, the guide to calling bids written by a committee of industry associations, could carry a note on issuing the template beside the PDF and naming which one governs. ISO 10845-3, the standard conditions of tender, could add the rule the book gives the buyer's Listener, the bot that collects bidders' questions: a question raised by any bidder's bot is answered to every bidder, by a new version of the template.

**Where the test goes.** AACE's estimate classes say how accurate an estimate can be expected to be at each stage of design; a conformance class beside them, agreement with a reference reading under a fixed protocol, is where the book's two to three percent belongs, since it measures something different from distance to the low bid. Since 2021, RICS has required its members to give every cost prediction an estimate of its accuracy, along with the sources of the data behind it. The test is the missing piece, and it has a working cousin next door. Property assessors price every house in a county with a model, and the International Association of Assessing Officers publishes how to score one against the sales that follow: is it right on the whole, how widely do its prices scatter, and does it price the expensive houses low? Each question has a published threshold, and a state can order a reappraisal when a county fails. The third question is the one that would catch a bot underpricing the largest contracts. On the buyer's side, IEEE 3119, published in 2025, walks a buyer through procuring an AI system; a construction profile could require that any bot the buyer relies on has passed the test. The castle is a new part of ISO 10845 holding the public test set and the tolerance, reached through a national standards body; whether Canada sits on its subcommittee is the first thing to find out.

**Where the sign-off goes.** This is the one with a head start, because professions already sign. Engineers seal drawings, and a highway engineer already signs a written justification when a low bid looks odd. RICS has written the AI duty for surveyors, and engineering regulators and estimating societies could write the same duty into their own practice standards, by the exposure drafts and public comment the actuaries use. Anyone may write an extension to the Open Contracting Data Standard, from a template the Open Contracting Partnership keeps with its registry. One for signed readings, and the questions and versions around them, needs nobody's permission, only work, and would put them beside the contract data governments already publish. Past all three is the courthouse, where Daubert in the United States and the cases that followed Mohan in Canada ask of expert evidence a method that can be tested and a known error rate, and a federal rule on machine-generated evidence is being redrafted. The standards are built so that both answers are on the shelf, with a name beside them, when the first case arrives.

The order is the pitch. The extension, the crosswalk and a profession's practice note need nobody's permission, the guide notes and profiles need a membership and a committee, and the castle needs a national body and years. Each level gives the next one something already running.

## Getting the standards to stick

Lang borrows his best line on failure from Carl Cargill, a veteran of technology standards work: most standards efforts do not fail in the committee. They fail before it, when nobody launches them, or after it, when the finished standard finds no one who wants it. In Lang's words, "the danger zone for standards is sometimes in the starting but always in the sticking." The old plan was weakest at sticking. It assumed that a good model would stick because it was good, which is the one thing Cargill's history says is not enough.

Sticking is also a waiting game. The courts will not lean on a standard that does not exist yet, a standards body will not write one without a pilot, and a buyer will not fund a pilot without a return it can count. The way out is a benefit one of them can capture now: the evaluator's savings, which arrive before any standard does. It is also why the first two standards are small. A template and a signature cost a buyer little, and they pile up the evidence the test will need.

Lang's 2026 Symposium talk on field building adds the rest of the checklist. Fields do not generate themselves; someone builds them, often an outsider. Name the thing enthusiastically and define it hesitantly, so that "the reasonable bot" can grow into its test set instead of being fixed too early. The book's two names split that advice between them. Bot Construction is the enthusiastic name, for the field that runs from the tender to the robot on site. Bot Procurement is the hesitant one, for the standards, and they will hold only what has been tested. Make artifacts: the estimator is one, the template is another, and so is this book. Recruit keystone people from neighbouring fields: someone who has sat on a standards committee, a construction lawyer who has argued over a reading, and the owner's staff who defend change-order prices in disputes. Beware capture: the likely captor is a platform vendor whose own format becomes the standard by default, which is the strongest reason to keep the template and the test free. The standard is public so anyone can run it. And find the economic flywheel a field cannot live without: here, the evaluator's savings, the claims never started and the reviews never paid for.

Then there is upkeep. Nadia Eghbal's *Working in Public* calls a project with many users and few maintainers a stadium, and its lasting cost is their attention, not the first build. The estimator is maintained by one person; if the plan works, it becomes a stadium. A liability standard is most fragile at that stage, because a court needs it to still be there, unchanged, when the claim arrives years later. The CubeSat took about ten years to take off for that reason; as one of its inventors, Jordi Puig-Suari, put it, "People needed to gain some comfort that these standards are going to be around for a while." New versions will need funding, and the pace of revisions will follow the value the market places on the standard. The way out of the stadium is the move Lang describes, into a consortium or a standards body where the people who rely on the standard share its upkeep. IFC, the open format for building models, shows how long that road can be. It began in 1994 as a club of twelve companies invited by one software vendor, and in 1996 it became a neutral alliance because the vendor's own users insisted the format should belong to no one. The buyers came later: the UK did not require shared building models on central government projects until 2016, and the version of IFC that covers roads and bridges became an ISO standard in 2024, thirty years after the first meeting. It is also a reason not to build the castle alone. The neighbouring bodies on the map already meet, and joining them may be faster than founding a new one; three standards make that easier, since the documents, the test and the sign-off can each go to a different neighbour. This book makes the case on its own only because a problem has to be described before anyone can be asked to share it. Owners can help before then: Kelly Roegies's guide to open protocols for governments suggests procurement criteria that reward suppliers who contribute upstream.

## The campaign for World 2

The versions in chapter 10 now line up with the three standards.

**Version 1.0, momentum.** One trade first, probably civil work, where public bid prices already exist. The template published for that trade, with its crosswalk to the pay items. Every reading signed, made on an offline model whose weights do not change, and carried by the OCDS extension. A small group of true believers, and this book as the vision paper.

**Version 2.0, proof of work.** The evaluator in an owner's hands, with a named person signing each reading it uses, and the first tenders issued with the template beside the PDF. The asks that need a committee go in: the CCDC note, the ISO 10845-3 clause, the IEEE profile, and a practice note from a profession that signs.

**Version 3.0, the working model.** By now the signed readings hold enough to build the test from, and it goes looking for its castle.

The prediction can be wrong, and it should say how. If by 2035 no public owner anywhere runs a tender whose questions, addenda, bids and evaluation pass through agents on a published data format, the forecast failed. The standard itself is further out, in the ten to fifteen years the CubeSat suggests, and its first court challenge will probably come before it, from a dispute fought under the old rules.

The model was never going to generate the field. What it can do is give a small group of people a template to issue, a reading to sign and a test to argue about. That is how a standard starts, and the villagers, as the zombie garden in chapter 9 promised, are the ones who decide whether it sticks.
