## The banana peel

Amber Case has a simple test for calm technology: the outside should tell you what is going on inside, the way a banana peel tells you when the fruit is ready. Tenders are unlikely ever to earn her designation. The peel is still a fair goal for the bots and for the standard they are read against.

The trouble is that a peel can be dressed up. Ask bidders to log what their bots found, or to sign a declaration that they raised everything, and the logs and declarations become something to manage. An unscrupulous bidder will follow the letter and not the spirit. A scrupulous one will follow both, and the buyer will read the tidy log as a sign that the documents had no defects. Either way the peel claims to know more about the fruit than it does.

The honest design admits the uncertainty and lets it change the price. The estimator is being built to work that way. A line it has seen too rarely is flagged for a person to price, never quietly priced. Every number will carry the size of the bot's usual miss beside it, so a reader can see how much the inside knows. A bidder who bids against a flagged line knows the risk is there, and so does the buyer.

That is what a sailor's consequences look like before the ship leaves the harbour. Nobody is promised a sober bot. Everyone is shown how far it tends to wander.

### Dressing up the banana

> By requiring logging and declarations we are creating temptations for bidders to dress up their bananas. This would happen even if outright deception was not used, because unscrupulous bidders could interpret the requirements to follow the letter but not the spirit. It would even happen if the scrupulous bidders followed the instructions, since these steps would be interpreted as signs that there are no defects in the requirements. Admitting that uncertainty exists, with consequences on pricing, avoids believing that the banana peel can tell us more information than it actually can.

(positions §16, recorded 2026-10-10)
