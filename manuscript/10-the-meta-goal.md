Frederick Taylor split the worker from the manager. Someone in an office would plan the work, and someone on the floor would do it. The gains were real and measurable, and in construction they have flattened out. The office kept growing anyway. Every estimate, schedule, submittal and change order needs someone at a desk, and the people who know the work best are often the ones least at home with the paperwork.

The goal behind this project began there. I put it in the case study this book grew from:

> The meta-goal of the study is increased autonomy for workers. Taylorism split the worker from the manager, with measurable productivity gains, but these gains have plateaued, so a new paradigm is needed. AI provides the means to build one, by empowering workers to self-perform a wide array of administrative tasks that currently siphon productivity towards staff positions.

## Autonomy in the doing

The first path runs through documents. If a bot can read the tender, take off the quantities and draft the price, the barrier that keeps a capable foreman from bidding a job of their own gets lower. A small crew can stay small and still be a principal on a large project, working beside other small crews like the surgical teams of chapter 8. The paperwork needed to grow stops being the reason not to.

That path is real, and it is narrower than it looks. Cheap paperwork gets a team in the door. A bid the owner can check on the same terms as a large firm's is what lets the team stay, which is why this book asks for a standard and not only for tools.

## Autonomy in the imagining

The second path runs further out. If procurement, design, monitoring and building can all be done with less human attention, people get that attention back. What they spend it on is the interesting part. Decisions will still be pulled upward as information moves faster, but less hierarchy would be needed to reach the same level of creativity. The human skill moves toward describing what we actually want: knowing ourselves well enough that the built world can fit us, instead of being built for resale.

These two goals are not rivals. They meet in one machine.

## The jigbot

Chapter 9 gave it its name. A jigbot is what a tradesperson builds when they set up and teach a robot for the task at hand, the way they'd build a jig.

Today's site robots are mostly programmed away from the site. Programming them means translating data from one format into another, with a check at every step. Documents written for machines would remove much of that translation. A robot could then program itself from the same documents the bidders priced, and the tradesperson's job would be to teach it the task at hand. That is how a better jigbot gets built: by the person who knows the cut, with the machine doing the rest.

The jigbot serves both goals at once. The worker who teaches it gains autonomy in the doing, because the machine answers to the person beside it and not to head office. Everyone gains autonomy in the imagining, because things that cannot be built today become affordable to try.

This is the destination, not a plan. The field this book belongs to is Bot Construction, and robots on site sit at its far end. Bot Procurement is the part that has to be earned first.

## Questions for the destination

Some questions belong to the field, not to the standard, and this book leaves them open.

Who answers for a jigbot? Probably the same three things that answer for a powder-actuated tool or a scissor lift today: a standard, training with a ticket to show for it, and insurance. The hard part is the width of the teaching. Makers will want the machine to do only what they planned, and the trades will need it to follow the site.

How many doors should a project have? Today one builder usually answers for everything behind the tender. Once machines can read the documents, a buyer might reach the trades more directly. Whether that helps depends on who stands behind each piece and who answers for how the pieces fit. That question belongs to all of them, not to one side.

## Hammurabi for bots

The prologue opened in Babylon, where the builder answered with his life. It is tempting to bring the rule back for bots. Let the bot that causes harm be switched off, and on a branded bot let the sentence fall on the brand: its code, its files and its weights.

It is a working rule, and it may even be where to start. But deleting a bot punishes nothing in the bot. It punishes whoever owned it, and a brand can be rebuilt overnight as a twin. A rule that makes the destruction of intelligence routine may also leak into how we treat each other. Keeping no standards and no consequences would teach the machines the fastest, and choosing a standard means choosing to learn more slowly and more safely.

## The keyholder

The Stevenson Test said New Nature is wherever there is nobody to appeal to. Bots will make a great deal of new nature. They will read faster than any of us, and they will be wrong in ways that are hard to argue with. What is left for people is to imagine what to build, to decide, and to appeal what was decided. That may be the most human work there is.

So the book ends with a person, not a bot. The keyholder reads what the machine drafted, adjusts the lines it flagged, signs, and gives a reason. Every chapter has been about making that signature cheaper to give and safer to rely on.

None of this is built by one person. The project hopes to arrive as a field: buyers, bidders, trades, designers, vendors, lawyers and insurers, each holding a piece of the jig.
