The question behind this book has not changed since the first page: when a bot causes financial harm, who pays? Lawyers answered with the chapters on liability and evidence. Underwriters put a price on harm before it happens, and this turned out to be more useful for the project. To underwrite a risk, an insurer needs to measure a risk. Insurers already measure AI products one at a time, but each evaluation needs its own custom validation, which makes it hard for a vendor to shop around for the best price. A reference standard would provide a baseline for measuring the deviation of AI-powered estimating products. The project thinks the insurable event is a shortfall in margin, measured across a broad spectrum of tenders rather than on one job. The bidder declares its costs on bid day, and the insurer keeps a right to audit the books as sureties already do. This is a proposal to test with underwriters, and the rest of this opening explains why.

## The insurable event

There are issues involving perverse incentives since the bidder ultimately controls costs and could make every line pay out. A clever bot may also suggest a low bid on purpose when the company's particular situation still allows a profit. A low bid is then a strategy and not an error, so it cannot be judged on bid day. Insuring the low bid itself would not work either. Every low bid that wins makes the bidder's finances worse, and tenders come too slowly for the data to arrive before several losses.

It may be possible to insure against insufficient margins instead. Lost margin is where an estimating bot's failure would hurt a bidder most, so it is the risk a bidder would want covered before relying on an outside AI. Anyone can bid low to get the job; the trick is still making money afterwards. The bot would be instructed to design a bid that meets a target margin while understanding the bidder's internal costs. The bidder declares those costs in the estimate file on bid day, such as owned equipment, crew rates or a nearby yard, and the margin is measured against a cost model built from them. Costs on site never enter the claim, so inflating them gains nothing. A false declaration voids the policy, and the insurer keeps a right to audit the books as sureties already do. The bidder also carries a share of any shortfall. The software would be deemed in default when its margins keep falling short across a broad spectrum of tenders, not when one job loses money. Character can still play a role, as it does with bid bonds, in the size of the coverage the insurer is willing to provide. It is not a finished product, and no insurer offers it today as far as the project has found.

## Four open questions

Four questions direct this effort, and the project cannot answer them alone:

### 1. Where does the product's normal error end and a defect begin?

As a probabilistic technology, bringing an AI-powered pricing bot's error to zero would lose the prediction model that drives its usefulness. Its strength lies in discovering averages and reaching solutions at superhuman speeds. The tails will always be there with a technology built on probabilities. Insurance can pick up the tails left over after the model tuning. Where would an underwriter draw the line between error that comes with the product and a defect?

### 2. Could insurance speed up adoption?

By picking up the tail risk, insurers will allow for faster iterations. Software developers and their clients could forgo a lengthy trial period and begin using productivity-enhancing tools much earlier in the lifecycle. This is great for the software client receiving the productivity gains, and great for the software developer receiving cash flow to develop the next iteration of the product. What would an insurer need to see before covering a tool that has not finished its trials?

### 3. How should the tails be covered?

With careful measurements and validation, it should be possible to insure a product based on its own characteristics, but the coverage of the tails would be tricky. Should the risk be capped leaving the vendor and their client exposed in the case of a major failure? Should the insurance premium be increased making deals less likely to close?

### 4. Would a shared standard lower insurance premiums?

Both of the questions about the tails are ameliorated through a reference standard. In our case of AI enhanced procurement, a standard AI-powered estimating system would provide a baseline insurers could use to limit payout risks. Since the reference standard would be able to generate estimates with error ranges, commercial products could be measured against this standard. Premiums could be capped by making the deviation a condition of each insurance policy. A bidder who prices below the standard is free to do so, but that bid is not covered. How much would a shared standard need to tell an insurer before it changes the price of a policy?

There is also the issue of bid inflation. By limiting coverage of bids that may be too low based on the standard, insurers will create incentives for keeping bids closer to the expected outcomes. There will be systematic risk to buyers from this approach. The carrot to counterbalance this risk would be the growth of custom bids. Alternative bids are tricky for people to judge, and they draw complaints from unsuccessful bidders because the selection criteria cannot be written in advance without knowing the nature of the value engineering. A working, open-source estimator solves this problem, because the method itself becomes the criterion disclosed in the tender. Bidders can model their proposals on their own copy before closing and see the outcomes, and the buyer judges them the same way afterwards. The same file always gets the same price, whoever runs it. A person still needs to review each alternative, since it may conflict with a need the tender never stated. The tender can say up front that alternatives pass or fail on the engineer's technical review, with written reasons, and a back-up lowest-cost bid applies if none of the alternatives is suitable. Life-cycle costs could come into a later version of the standard. Standardized AI could then replace the incentives for bid inflation with the opportunity for contractors to use their expertise to offer solutions unforeseen in the tender.

With these in mind, the aim is to make a probabilistic price insurable inside a bidding system that pretends every price is certain. The courts would not need to move for this to happen. Underwriters would.

## The standards we are following

The project does not need to invent a framework for AI. Several already exist, and an underwriter or a lawyer will look for them first. The plan is to follow their lead and add only what they leave out. None of them says how to check whether a price is right. The project's standard adds three things. The first is an agreed way to measure how far an AI estimate misses. The second is a fair point of comparison, which here is what contractors actually bid. The third is a range around that comparison, wide enough that an honest bid rarely falls outside it, which an insurer can write a policy against.

1. NIST AI Risk Management Framework (United States). This is the backbone. It lists about 72 practices for mapping, measuring and managing the risks of an AI system. Its text is free to quote and adapt anywhere, so the project's standard can be written as a profile under it without anyone buying a copy. Texas already requires its state agencies to align their AI use with it.
2. EU AI Act, Article 15. A tender bot is not a high-risk system under the Act, but Article 15 is the only clause in the frameworks we read that asks a provider to declare its accuracy and the metrics behind it. It is the precedent for publishing how far the estimates miss and the range an honest bid should fall within.
3. ISO/IEC 42001. This is a certifiable management system for the organization that runs the AI, not for the AI itself. It is a paid standard, so the project cites its clause numbers rather than its text. Several of its controls match the estimate file.
4. MITRE ATLAS. This is a catalogue of attacks on AI systems. The one that matters most here is poisoned data, such as fake or rigged bid results fed into the price history.
5. RICS professional standard on AI. The Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors made it mandatory for its members on 9 March 2026. Surveyors must keep a register of the AI they use, and a named surveyor must decide whether an AI output is reliable enough to use. It is the one construction body already setting rules for AI, and lawyers expect courts to read it when judging negligence.

Insurers already work this way with models they did not build. American actuaries follow a written rule, ASOP 56, on how far they may rely on someone else's model. Florida has accepted private hurricane models against published standards since 1995. An AI estimating system could be listed the same way.

Following their lead has three advantages. A reader from insurance, law or IT security sees familiar ground and can check the profile against what they already use. The project can borrow years of consensus instead of building it from scratch. A crosswalk to each framework also lets a commercial product show that it meets the project's standard and theirs at the same time. None of these frameworks sets a number, though, and following them is evidence of care rather than a shield. Deciding who pays is still left to the policy and the contract.

## The players on the field

Two organizations are already insuring AI, and both are a few years ahead of this project. Neither is working on construction pricing.

AIUC. The Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company is a venture-funded firm founded in 2025. It wrote AIUC-1, a standard for AI agents with about fifty requirements across six areas: data, security, safety, reliability, accountability and society. Products are audited against it, certified for a year and retested every quarter, and the firm arranges insurance for certified products. Several of its accountability requirements describe the estimate file almost exactly: a named owner for each change, approval before release, and tamper-evident logs of every input and output.

Its strength is speed, which came from building on existing frameworks instead of starting from scratch. Within about seven months it had a standard built on NIST, the EU AI Act and MITRE, an accredited auditor and a first insured customer. It built consensus by interviewing hundreds of security and risk leaders before writing. Its weaknesses for this project are just as clear. It was written for chatbots and agents, so much of it, such as prompt injection and jailbreaks, does not apply to a weighted median of past prices. It sets no accuracy threshold and runs no statistical test. One company owns the text, the tests and the certificate, and its terms forbid copying or adapting the standard.

So the project should keep going on its own. AIUC-1 was written for a different kind of risk, such as a chatbot giving a wrong or harmful answer. Few AI products price and bid on someone's behalf, so nothing in it measures whether a price is fair. In this project the price is the product, and its accuracy is what drives adoption. That needs a fair point of comparison and an agreed range around it, which AIUC-1 does not have. Joining would mean writing under terms that rule out an open text, which is the main thing this project offers. The better path is a crosswalk that cites AIUC-1's requirement numbers, so a vendor could hold both certificates without doing the work twice.

Munich Re and Armilla. One of the world's largest reinsurers, Munich Re has insured the performance of AI models since about 2018. Its process starts by agreeing a metric with the client, followed by a technical questionnaire, a risk report and a premium set by the risk. The guarantee is that the error stays below an agreed threshold, and the payout grows with the shortfall. This is close to what insurance for bot procurement could look like. The agreed error would be the margin the bot promised, and the shortfall would be measured across all of the vendor's clients. The provider locks its predictions and checks them against what happens later, which is close to how this project plans to test its own tools on newer contracts. Armilla, a smaller firm in Toronto that writes policies through Lloyd's, works in a similar way. It assesses a model before insuring it and includes an independent certification with every policy. Its main product covers financial loss that an AI causes to third parties. That is a separate space, since most problems in a tender are between two parties, the buyer and the bidder.

Munich Re's strength is that it already pays claims on performance alone, with no need to prove anyone was negligent. Its method works on any kind of model, and its own material says no model can ever be perfect. The weakness of both firms is that each evaluation is custom. The metric is agreed client by client, the method is public only in outline, and two quotes from two insurers may not measure the same thing. Neither firm knows much about tendering law or the gap between a bid and a cost.

So the project is not a competitor to either firm. It does not sell insurance and has no financial interest in doing so. What it can offer is the common point of comparison that their processes have to rebuild for every client. A public way to measure the misses, a public test, and an agreed range for construction pricing would make each evaluation cheaper and each quote comparable, and it would give any insurer, not only one, something to write a policy against.

Many market issues are still open. There is no loss history yet that links a low bid to real losses. Many bidders using the same standard could all fail at once. The bidders keenest to buy coverage may be the riskiest ones, and an open range can be gamed. The market may also be too small for an insurer to build a product alone. The project is not meant to solve these. It exists to show that the conversation is worth having.

Not every bidder needs this. A small company can hold its whole picture in one or two heads: its equipment, its crews, its costs and how much risk it will take. A vanilla AI or a home-built tool already gives it a bid to review, and paying for software and insurance on top would turn profit into overhead. Medium and large companies are different. Their estimating departments have middle managers and span regions, and they bid on the largest jobs, where a losing bid wastes the most effort. For them, insured bot procurement is a way to standardize estimates and raise productivity while keeping risk under control. These are the bidders a pilot should start with.

## What we need now: the ranges an off-the-shelf policy would require

The project would like input from underwriters on the ranges and variance an off-the-shelf insurance policy for estimating bots would require. How far off may a bot's price be, how often, and how steady must that be before an insurer could sell a standard policy rather than a custom one?

## What we need later: a pilot, then a standard

The ask will change as the tools improve, roughly in three steps.

1. Once the reference Estimator and Model are good enough, the next step is a pilot with a real insurance policy. One insurer would write a small, capped policy on the reference tools, used by a few willing bidders on real tenders. The pilot would produce real claims or near misses, and policy terms that have been tested in practice. A lower-stakes start could be pricing change orders after a contract is awarded, where no bid is at risk. A draft term sheet for such a pilot is in [Appendix E](app-e-pilot-term-sheet.md), for an underwriter to mark up.
2. The pilot then feeds the standard. Large-scale adoption needs firmer limits on liability than any single policy can give, and an industry standard is where those limits get written. The policy terms tested in the pilot would become its first requirements.
3. With the standard in place, insurers could cover any product that passes the same public test. Private companies and programmers could then walk through the door, if they find the market attractive enough. They can build much better estimating software than this project, and the reference tools would stay only as the floor everyone is measured against.

The reference tools do not need to be the best. They need to be good enough to run the pilot, build the standard, and build the market.

What would an insurer gain from the pilot? Not a proprietary product. Whatever the pilot teaches would go into an open standard, and that may cool some appetite for the experiment. What the insurer would gain is first-hand experience with the technology and the standard before anyone else, which is a real first-mover advantage. It would also help build a field that bidders are willing to enter, because they could finally put a value on the insurance. A proprietary product would be worth more to one firm, but a field is far more likely to grow into a market, to the benefit of the first mover, with latecomers trying to keep up.
