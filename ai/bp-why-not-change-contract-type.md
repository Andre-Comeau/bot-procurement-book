# Why not change the contract type instead?

> Outline. Every contract type moves the pricing risk somewhere; none makes it disappear.

## In short

- **Lump sum** (hard bid, in design-bid-build or design-build): the price is fixed before the work is fully known, and the builder carries the risk of getting it wrong. It works when the buyer knows what it wants, how to ask for it, and what it should cost. It needs some degree of adversarial relationship.
- **Cost plus:** the builder works for the buyer and is paid its costs plus a fee. The scope and price float, and the buyer pays every cost. With a percentage fee, each extra dollar spent earns the builder a little more.
- **Progressive design-build:** the team is chosen for its qualifications, and the price is agreed with open books one package at a time. As of 2024 only sixteen US jurisdictions had a law allowing it for public roads. Open books help only if someone on the buyer's side can read them.
- **The common thread:** each type still needs someone to judge a price, at the bid or after it, which is the gap Bot Procurement addresses ([the change-pricing problem](bp-change-pricing-problem.md)).

## What the evidence says about lump sums

- **Where quantities are uncertain, a lump sum costs more,** because each bidder carries the quantity risk and prices it like an insurer. A model of 440 Massachusetts bridge projects (1998 to 2015) estimated that asking for lump sums instead of unit prices would have raised the department's spending by 133 percent on average and 85 percent on the middle project (Bolotnyy and Vasserman, 2023).
- **On simple, well-defined work the gap shrinks and can reverse.** Florida keeps lump sums for work such as fencing, signs and resurfacing. A 2007 study of about 2,400 of its contracts found lump sums about four points dearer at award than unit-price contracts, measured against the department's estimate, and about two and a half points cheaper by the end, because they grew far less during construction.
- **Unit prices move the reading risk to the owner without removing it.** In the Massachusetts data, bidders priced higher on items whose quantities later overran, by about 0.085 percent for each 1 percent of overrun.
