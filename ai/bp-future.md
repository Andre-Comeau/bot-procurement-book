# Where AI construction procurement leads: from Bot Procurement to Bot Construction

> Outline. Robotic construction is the far-off land the project sails towards, not a plan with steps.

## In short

- **The destination:** Bot Construction, the field. At its far end, robots on site take direction from other programs, from other robots and fleet agents as well as from a person with a controller.
- **The road:** pricing and checking tenders (Bot Procurement), then documents written for machines, then bot administration of changes, payments and records, then robots on site ([the field and the standard](bp-two-names.md)).
- **The idea behind it:** building models are underused by people and ripe for bots as purely digital models; robot control can be an extension of the model. Both would spread faster if bots were common in construction and the information had common standards.
- **Growing out of the trades:** the destination is written as growing out of the trades' own work, not against it ([stakeholders: the trades](bp-stakeholder-trades.md)).

## The open question: one door or many

The project takes one interface, the tender, as given ([the tender as the interface](bp-tender-as-interface.md)). A general contractor already prices the work from trades' quotes. Once machines can read the documents, could a buyer reach the trades directly, and who would stand behind each piece and behind how the pieces fit? The book asks this as a question about the field, not as a plan to split anyone's margin. The evidence is mixed: New York has required separate trade contracts on public buildings for most of a century (the Wicks Law), and studies of its cost disagree.

## The end state: the jigbot, and what it gives people

The book's term for the end state comes from the trades: a jigbot is what a tradesperson builds when they set up and teach a robot for the task at hand, the way they'd build a jig. It is a plain word, not a third name for the field ([stakeholders: the trades](bp-stakeholder-trades.md)).

The author's position (November 2026) is that the jigbot serves two goals at once, both reached indirectly rather than aimed at directly:

- **Worker autonomy.** The project's first statement of its deeper goal was more autonomy for workers: tools that remove the documentation barrier keeping field workers out of principal roles. A tradesperson who teaches the machine decides how the work is done.
- **Attention for imagining.** If procurement, design, monitoring and building need little human attention, people have more attention for working out what they want and what the details mean, with less hierarchy needed for the same creativity.

Bot Procurement does not deliver either goal itself. It is the first step on the road, kept small enough to test.

## Aside: the question at the end of the chain

If a robot builds a model's error, who answers? It is the same question Bot Procurement asks of a price ([the liability gap](bp-liability-gap.md)), one step further down the road.
