## 1. The review (Claude's)

The argument fits the project's record well. The fixes needed are mostly in the opening paragraphs: several
attributions that the Institute's readers will know better than anyone.

### Where it matches the record

- **The PDF aside** is decision 17.

  His conclusion there was to work on both. They are also the sixth tension on the bot-first protocol card.
- **Standardization as the answer** is side quest 1, and his line "standardizing the complement".

### Attributions to fix

1. **"Stevenson's Law":** no source uses that name. The Institute's blog calls it the **Stevenson Test**:
   New Nature is where there is nobody to appeal to. In Sean Stevenson's Symposium talk (23 September
   2026), losing the right of appeal is one sign among several, not the defining one.
2. **"PSY-RG":** the Institute's site lists **SIGPSY, the Special Interest Group in Psychohistory**, under
   its "Research Groups" heading.
   - Venkatesh Rao and Aneesh Sathe lead it, and it maintains worldmachines.org.
   - The Prime Radiant is Sathe's own project, tied to World Machines.
3. **"discovered New Nature":** New Nature is Rao's term.
   - He introduced it in a Protocolized post ("Theorizing Protocolization I: New Nature") and wrote it up
     on Contraptions on 16 January 2026.
   - It was the theme of the Protocol Town Hall's fourth season.
   - The Prime Radiant charts machines from 1200 onward; it didn't find New Nature.
4. **"pre-commodification re-wilds the world":** the closest source found is Sathe's essay "The Birth of
   Thickets".
   - In it a thicket is open ground that resists enclosure, and "pre-commodified" is credited to another
     writer.
   - AI appears there as the force that averages ideas and flattens them: in that essay AI is what a thicket
     escapes, not what makes it grow. So the draft's sentence may run the other way.
   - Read only through a page summary (○); worth checking in full, along with its sequel, "On Thickets,
     Enclosure, and Leaving".

### Two smaller points of law

- **Hammurabi's rule never fully went away.** The same card shows France's ten-year guarantee (1804) and
  Quebec's five-year presumption (Civil Code article 2118): the builder is still liable without proof of
  fault. Only the death penalty was dropped. This supports "in common law jurisdictions": civil law kept the
  presumption, and common law took up the reasonable person.
- **"A single legal fiction":** in tenders the test is the reasonable *bidder*.
  - Professionals are held to the standard of a reasonably competent member of their profession.
  - His own line, "'Reasonable bidder' is still being defined. 'Reasonable bot' has yet to be tested."
    (`context-bank/bot-procurement-process.md`), would tie in neatly.

### Where it goes past the record

- **A court using the standard bot:** the record has two pieces.
  - His point that a standard settles which reading binds, which a better reader cannot supply (bot-first
    protocol card).
  - Claude's note that the law asks what a reasonable bidder would have understood.

  No law has been checked for either, and `context-bank/legal-assumptions.md` has no entry for it. If the
  essay relies on it, an entry with references should be added.

  It may also help the essay: Stevenson predicts courts will treat bad outcomes from AI like acts of God,
  insured against rather than sued over. The standard bot answers him by keeping an appeal open.
- **The cost side of §13 is missing.** He recorded that the consultants' checking costs fall on the Owner
  unless bidders save more than that, and that bidders stay free to use other bots. The draft gives only
  the benefits, and the tensions are the hook in his case study.
- **Worker autonomy,** the meta-goal at the top of the README, doesn't appear yet.
- **Timing:** his working assumption is that the standard takes effect, and may first be tested in court,
  10 to 15 years out (2036 to 2041).

## 2. The draft, lightly copyedited (Andre's words)

Where a fix needed his own words, his sentence is left as written and marked with a letter; the notes
follow the text.

---

The Stevenson Test states that nature does not allow for appeals. Civilizations are the product of world
machines humanity has built to generate systems for appeals.

Venkatesh Rao, Aneesh Sathe and SIGPSY (the Special Interest Group in Psychohistory), with the World
Machines and Prime Radiant projects, have been mapping out humanity's recent efforts and discovered New
Nature: an AI-powered phenomenon where pre-commodification re-wilds the world. **[a]** In this jungle, a
small sliver of light fell onto my industry, left behind on the forest floor as tech-based businesses
reached for the sky.

Along with agriculture, construction has concerned itself with providing the physical backing needed to
drive the creation of increasingly complex machines. Unlike agriculture, which provides uncertain outputs
that are easy for outsiders to measure, construction has been plagued since the days of Hammurabi by its
need to provide certain outcomes that are hard for the non-builder to evaluate. The Babylonians resolved
the issue with the simplest solution: strict liability for the builder. The homeowner could appeal for the
builder to be liable for all damages; the builder could not appeal.

While our society's lack of appetite for murdering the sons of general contractors in eye-for-an-eye legal
remedies is welcomed by construction workers and their families, moving away from the Mesopotamian
solution to the principal-agent problem has required a far more complex framework.

These days the tension is distributed through multiple protocols: training, certification, inspection,
licensing, engineering, claims, and contracts. This system for resolving appeals and counterappeals has
developed over thousands of years and, in common law jurisdictions, resists New Nature through a single
legal fiction: the reasonable person. **[b]**

The Gramsci monster in our story, the one that spawns in the time between machines, is the reasonable bot.

The current tech stack allows AI bots to design buildings, write specifications, read specifications, and
bid on requirements. They may not be able to do this well, but the tech keeps improving. When it reaches
parity with human estimators, the challenge will come not from calculations but from liability. How could
the productivity gains from the automation of the administrative portions of construction procurement be
realized if humans must always review the work of bots?

For long-time followers of the Protocol Institute, the answer may have been obvious: standardization. For
those who, like me, needed to take the long route, a mini-Prime Radiant project focused on Bot Procurement
may be needed to arrive at the same answer: [Procurement Machines](link).

AI-first procurement will need two tools: **[c]**

* a shift away from the primacy of human-centric documentation to standardized instructions generated for
  machine readability. (Yes, for those in the know, it would mean getting rid of PDFs — no one really liked
  looking at paper sheets on a screen anyhow.)
* a standardized bot for reading the specifications.

With these tools, engineers and architects could still certify the canonical copy, the court could use the
standardized bot as a surrogate for a reasonable bot, and bidders would be able to lower their estimating
costs. **[d]**

---
