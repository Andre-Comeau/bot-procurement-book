# Outline

A map of the book in reading order, one short paragraph per chapter.

**The question.** AI can now price and check construction bids. What has to exist before buyers, bidders, insurers and courts can rely on it? The book's answer is a proposal, Bot Procurement: a public standard for how such a bot is tested and recorded, paired with insurance for the bidder's margin.

## Overview. Why do we need a standard?

**Prologue.** How the project started: a working estimator's view of tendering, the tool built to understand it, and why the answer turned out to be a standard rather than a product.

## Part I. What is the problem?

**1. The tender and the flood of AI bids.** A tender is the interface between a buyer and every bidder. AI floods that interface with polished bids that say less than they used to, and the old ways of reading them cannot keep up.

**2. Tensions built into the tender.** A design fixed before anyone prices it, a long and costly bid, and the bidder who prices the errors and claims for them later.

## Part II. How should we solve it?

**3. Insuring the bidder's margin.** The proposed solution: insure a shortfall in margin across many tenders, measured against costs declared on bid day, and follow the AI frameworks that underwriters and lawyers already use. Along the way: who answers when a bot misreads a tender, and why a bidder should not be forced to report what its bot finds.

## Part III. Why not use other tools? Why not change the contract type?

**4. Why does the hard bid survive?** How a public buyer judges whether a price is fair and reasonable today, from the engineer's estimate to price books and bid tabulations, and why the sealed low bid keeps winning.

**5. Where bids already travel as data.** Where bids already travel as data, in Germany and on US highways: who measures, who carries the mistakes, and why a file format helps but is not enough.

**6. Bigger ideas set aside for now.** The bigger approaches set aside, and why.

## Part IV. What do the stakeholders stand to gain?

**7. The buyers: more bids than they can read.** Five kinds of buyer, from the highway agency to the private owner, and what each needs when bids multiply.

**8. The bidders: what a check and cover change.** The bidder's stake, the firms the project is and is not for, and what a standard and insurance would change for them.

**9. The trades: who sets up the machines.** What changes for the trades when bots price and robots build, and why the people who set up the tools should own them.

**10. The designers and consultants: checking the documents before issue.** Architects, engineers and testers: independent bots that check the documents before they are issued, so fewer questions reach the bidders.

**11. The software vendors: why a shared test suits them.** The software makers, from the big platforms to the startups, who carries the risk under their AI terms today, and why a shared standard suits them.

**12. What a legal department needs before relying on a bot.** What a court needs before it can rely on a bot's reading: admissible is not the same as reliable.

**13. The insurers: a pilot, then a standard.** Who already insures AI, what an off-the-shelf policy would require, and three steps from a pilot to a standard.

## Part V. How are we going to get there?

**14. How AI fails, and a remedy for each.** How AI fails in procurement: four kinds of failure, deliberate or accidental, in words or in deeds, each with a remedy.

**14a. A bot should show what it does not know.** The outside should tell you what is going on inside: why bots should admit their uncertainty, with consequences for the price.

**15. What AI gets right.** The PDF tender as a protocol that will not die, and what AI makes possible once it is read properly.

**16. The estimator and the evaluator: a second opinion on every price.** The project's estimator and the fair and reasonable evaluator: one engine pointed in two directions, built and tested in public.

**17. Three standards, and how they could stick.** How standards are made by small groups of people, the three standards the book proposes (the documents, the tool test and the sign-off), and how they could stick.

**17a. One bot failure, from tender to court.** One failure told from start to finish, from a quantity error in a tender to the claim in court.

## Part VI. How have things changed in the past?

**18. Two centuries of tendering.** Measure, price in sealed envelopes, turn the results into next year's price book: the loop tendering has run for two hundred years, and where it is fraying.

## Part VII. Where does this end up?

**19. The goal: more autonomy for the people who build.** What the tools could give back to the people who build: room for judgment, and a path from the field into the roles that decide.
